From kinematic to dynamic waves

Understand which physics are retained and how they are computed over cells and time steps. Compare the approaches, then open the topics relevant to your analysis.

Separate two choices

01 · Equations and the meaning of each term

Why shallow-water equations?

Shallow refers to depth relative to the horizontal variation length, not just a small absolute depth. Depth averaging and a hydrostatic approximation reduce the role of vertical acceleration. Deep rivers can meet these assumptions; strongly vertical jets and circulation need additional treatment.

∂A/∂t + ∂Q/∂x = ql

1D continuity: area A [m²], discharge Q [m³/s], lateral inflow per unit length qₗ [m²/s].

∂q/∂t + ∂(q²/h)/∂x + gh ∂h/∂x = gh(S0 − Sf)

Illustrative momentum equation for a simple constant-width channel, omitting lateral momentum and additional forces. q = hu is unit-width discharge [m²/s], u velocity, h depth, S₀ bed slope and S_f signed friction slope.

∂q/∂t
Local inertia: unit-width discharge changes over time at a location.
∂(q²/h)/∂x
Advective acceleration: water moves into a region with different velocity.
gh ∂h/∂x
Pressure gradient due to depth differences; together with bed slope it gives the water-surface gradient.

Kinematic flow uses S_f ≈ S₀. Diffusion wave retains the surface-gradient balance but drops accelerations. Local inertia restores temporal acceleration; dynamic wave also retains advection. Spatial dimension (1D/2D) is a separate choice.

Theory references: USACE · Kinematic wave · Clawpack · Shallow-water equations

Flow classification and backwater

Separate spatial and temporal changes

Uniform/nonuniform flow describes spatial variation at a given time; steady/unsteady flow describes temporal variation at a given location. Steady flow can be nonuniform. Kinematic routing uses a uniform-flow approximation but still computes time-varying discharge and storage through continuity.

Subcritical flow, supercritical flow and hydraulic jumps

Subcritical, critical and supercritical flow are flow regimes with Froude numbers below, equal to and above one in basic open-channel flow. They are distinct from upstream/downstream locations. A hydraulic jump is a transition from supercritical to subcritical flow with a rapid depth increase and energy dissipation. Gradually and rapidly varied flow describe the rate of spatial change.

Backwater does not necessarily imply reverse flow

Backwater is an upstream rise in water level caused by downstream stage or controls such as structures. It differs from drainage and does not necessarily involve flow reversal. Assess supercritical-flow and hydraulic-jump applications using the selected solver and relevant validation cases.

Theory reference: USACE · Mixed flow regimes

03 · Computing fluxes and storage on a mesh

Finite volumes and finite differences

Finite volumes update a cell’s conserved amount from boundary fluxes. A shared interface flux cancels in the sum over neighboring cells. Finite differences approximate derivatives from neighboring values and can also be conservative; the names alone do not rank accuracy.

Vin+1 = Vin + Δt (Qin − Qout + Qsource)

Illustrative conservative update of volume V [m³] using discharge Q [m³/s]. Sum signed flows over all faces when a cell has multiple boundaries.

Mass conservation alone does not make momentum representations equivalent. Kinematic routing closes discharge with a storage relation; local inertia uses previous flow and surface gradients; Full SWE also updates momentum fluxes.

Resolution and numerical diffusion

Reconstruction and flux evaluation affect how strongly sharp changes are smeared. Higher-order reconstruction can improve smooth regions, while limiting is needed near abrupt changes. Continued change under refinement indicates that resolution convergence has not yet been established.

04 · Explicit, implicit and semi-implicit time stepping

Explicit methods evaluate updates from known states. Implicit methods include new-time unknowns and require a coupled or nonlinear solve. Semi-implicit methods mix treatments across terms. These names describe time evaluation, not different physical processes.

(yn+1 − yn)/Δt = f(yn)

Forward Euler: an illustrative explicit time step.

(yn+1 − yn)/Δt = f(yn+1)

Backward Euler: an illustrative implicit time step, not a claim that every K-DRUM mode uses this formula.

Implicit stepping can still smear peaks and timing with large steps, and nonlinear iterations must converge. Explicit stepping must respect propagation relative to cell size. Stable completion does not establish temporal accuracy.

C = (|u| + √(gh)) Δt / Δx

Illustrative Courant number for 1D shallow-water waves. Limits depend on the method; 2D geometry, sources and boundaries may impose additional restrictions.

Reduce the timestep and compare peak, arrival time and balance at key locations. Forcing intervals, internal steps and output intervals can differ; output timestamps do not reveal every internal step.

06 · Boundaries, wetting/drying fronts and verification

Boundary conditions connect the model to its surroundings

Inflow discharge, downstream stage, tide, walls and open boundaries provide different information. Subcritical flow can transmit downstream-stage influence upstream; inflow alone does not determine stage. Boundary number and type must suit the flow regime and solver.

Wetting and drying require nonnegative depth and water conservation

Excessive outflow from a shallow cell can produce negative depth. Available-water limits and wet/dry handling are needed; velocity near a depth threshold is sensitive because it divides discharge by small depth. Clipping depth alone is not a conservation check.

  1. Lake at rest: does varying terrain create artificial flow in a resting state?
  2. Conservation: do boundary fluxes, sources, losses and storage change balance?
  3. Propagation and fronts: how sensitive are arrival times and wetting fronts to grid and timestep?
  4. Observations: have stage, discharge and inundation been compared with independent data?

These are criteria for assessing a simulation, not a claim that every listed capability has passed every test.

Further theory on surface gradients and discretization: USACE · Diffusion-wave solver. External references explain general theory, not identical K-DRUM implementation or performance.

07 · Cell hydrology: infiltration, storage, ET and snow

Different stores play different roles within one cell

K-DRUM links surface and A/B/C/D-layer storage and transfer. Rain and melt supply infiltration or surface runoff; soil water supplies ET and subsurface flow. The D layer is an existing deep store whose delayed return contributes to post-storm response. Interlayer transfers cancel in a whole-domain budget, while ET, outlet export and external deep loss are separate boundary terms.

ΔSdomain = P + Iexternal − Qout − ET − Lexternal

All terms refer to a common period/domain in volume [m³], or depth [mm] on a consistent area basis.

Capacity is not the same as actual infiltration

Green–Ampt uses wetting-front suction and the moisture difference across the front to estimate infiltration capacity. Suction matters relatively more at small cumulative infiltration; conductivity becomes dominant later. Actual entry cannot exceed water supplied at the surface. The storage increment and remaining surface excess must reconcile with the original supply.

f_c = Kₛ [1 + ψf Δθ/F]; ΔFinfil ≤ min(f_cΔt, Wsurface)

Representative expression: Kₛ/f_c [length/time], F/ψf/Wsurface [length]. Actual ponding and initialization are treated separately.

ET and melt are tied to available storage

ET links atmospheric demand to vegetation/root distribution but remains limited by available soil water. Empirical snow/melt calculations update water-equivalent storage with temperature and terrain/time modifiers; snow-water equivalent differs from physical snow depth. FAO is cited for ET concepts, not to claim every K-DRUM demand formula is identical to FAO-56.

SWEⁿ⁺¹ = SWEⁿ + snowfall − melt

All terms are interval water-equivalent depths. Melt transfers water into hydrology rather than removing it from the domain.

08 · The implemented 1D hydraulic calculation

Hydraulic metrics are derived from cross sections

Section X–Z coordinates define wetted area A, top width T, wetted perimeter P, hydraulic radius R=A/P and hydrostatic integral I₁ at stage η. Relative measured shape is retained and placed on the longitudinal bed. Changing width or bed elevation alters storage, conveyance and overflow conditions; it is not merely a visual adjustment.

A(η) = ∫ max(η − z(s), 0) ds; c = √(gA/T)

A [m²], T/P/R [m], c [m/s]. c is the section-dependent free-surface wave speed.

Dynamic waves retain mass and advective momentum

The current dynamic-wave route combines a finite-volume spatial operator with network connectivity. Shared mass flux enters neighboring cells with opposite signs. Momentum flux includes Q²/A advection and gI₁ hydrostatic pressure, together with non-prismatic geometry and friction sources. HLLE-family fluxes and limited reconstruction options concern numerical stability/accuracy, not a different physical equation.

d(UᵢΔxᵢ)/dt = Fᵢ₋½ − Fᵢ₊½ + SᵢΔxᵢ; U=(A,Q)ᵀ

Conservative overview; non-prismatic pressure/geometry terms must not be replaced indiscriminately by a rectangular-channel formula.

Separate implicit time stepping from nonlinear solution

Backward Euler and TR-BDF2 are connected to the present implicit 1D route. Backward Euler solves a new-time residual; TR-BDF2 combines a trapezoidal intermediate stage with a BDF2 stage. Nonlinear iterations reevaluate states, geometry and fluxes, solve linearized systems and reduce residuals. Implicit stability does not eliminate timestep sensitivity in peak magnitude or arrival time.

R(Xⁿ⁺¹)=M(Xⁿ⁺¹)−M(Xⁿ)−Δt F(Xⁿ⁺¹)=0; J δX=−R

Implicit residual and Newton-correction concept: X state, M conserved quantities, J residual Jacobian.

The network connects junctions and external boundaries

Branches/confluences require consistent flow signs, junction storage and ownership of connected faces. Upstream flow, lateral input, reservoir release and downstream stage are distinct boundary inputs and must not be applied twice. Local-inertial paths omit advective momentum; the DWNET name alone does not identify the equations. Implementation completion remains separate from a particular basin’s observation-based validation.

09 · 2D local inertia and Full SWE methods

Local inertia retains previous flow and surface-gradient forcing

Local inertia retains local acceleration but omits advective momentum. A face’s unit-width discharge q combines previous flow, surface-gradient acceleration and friction damping. The representative semi-implicit friction update below is used with effective face depth, terrain and available-volume handling. Both horizontal face-flow directions contribute to the same storage update.

qⁿ⁺¹ = [qⁿ − g hf Δt (ηR−ηL)/Δx] / [1 + g n² Δt |qⁿ|/hf⁷ᐟ³]

q [m²/s], hf/η/Δx [m], Δt [s]. This representative form excludes the separate details of dry-face handling and multidirectional flow.

Face discharge updates cell volume

Multiplying unit-width q by face length gives volumetric discharge Q. Net interval volumes update cell storage, from which depth/stage follows the terrain–storage relationship. Rainfall, drainage and river exchange enter as sources/sinks on the same interval. Excluded cells and impermeable faces are different conditions and are represented separately.

Vᵢⁿ⁺¹=Vᵢⁿ+Δt[Σf σᵢf qf Lf + Qsource,ᵢ]

V [m³], Lf [m]; σ is positive for inflow to a cell and negative for outflow.

Full SWE also evolves horizontal advective momentum

Full SWE evolves depth h and both unit-width discharges hu/hv. HLL-family interface calculations update advective and hydrostatic momentum fluxes along with mass, with terrain/friction sources. HLL approximates the Riemann problem using a bounding wave-speed range. Reconstruction and time order must be assessed with resolution, wet/dry and boundary handling, using supported grid/coupling configurations.

F(U)=(hu, hu²+gh²/2, huv)ᵀ; G(U)=(hv, huv, hv²+gh²/2)ᵀ

h [m], u/v [m/s]. These plan-view 2D fluxes differ from pressure integrals for arbitrary 1D sections.

Check wetting/drying and timestep with conservation

Outflow beyond the water available in a shallow cell would create negative depth. Available volume, effective depth, substepping and retry handling must work together. Completion alone does not establish rapid-flow accuracy; compare depth, velocity, front movement and balance. Multi-resolution patches aggregate or retain source terrain rather than generating new terrain detail.

Δt ≲ C Δx/(|u|+√(gh))

Representative shallow-wave timing relation; 2D directions, sources and coupling can impose additional limits. C is not assumed universal across modes.

10 · 1D–2D exchange, conservation and geometry

Both domains share one accepted exchange volume

River head, floodplain head and interface crest determine overflow or return. The time-integrated exchange is removed from one domain and added to the other. Directional gross volumes may be reported as positive magnitudes but differ from net transfer. Exchange closure, full-domain water balance and momentum conservation are distinct tests.

Vnet = V₁→₂ − V₂→₁; εexchange = ΔVriver + ΔVflood

All quantities are volumes [m³] over the same accepted interval.

Separate coupling trials from final acceptance

The current bidirectional route preserves interval-start states and iterates hydraulic response and exchange. Each trial must use the same starting state; otherwise repeated trials would move the same water repeatedly. Final exchange and stage response are checked before jointly accepting the two domains, with consistent retries for unacceptable intervals. Outputs use accepted states and transfer.

Sections, DEM and banks must describe the same physical interface

Measured sections can include submerged channels absent from surface DEMs. Compare 1D bed/bank geometry with 2D terrain using a common datum. Arbitrarily modifying crests or inserting fabricated sections to avoid solver difficulties is not validation. Distinguish illustrative sample geometry from measured application data.

Choose the actual solver combination explicitly

Distinguish bidirectional 1D dynamic-wave/2D local-inertial coupling from 1D local inertia, selectable Full SWE and terrain-based inundation screening. The existence of a capability does not imply every solver/boundary combination. Completed core functionality is therefore presented with its supported configuration scope.

11 · Structures, reservoirs and calibration

Hydraulic structure laws differ from operating decisions

Structure hydraulics calculates throughflow from heads, geometry and opening. Reservoir operation chooses releases using stage/storage, capacity, minimum flow and ramp constraints. Forecast pre-release and multi-reservoir scenarios extend this decision-support layer; they do not grant real-facility control or substitute for operational approval.

Vⁿ⁺¹ = Vⁿ + Δt(Qin − Qrelease − Qloss)

Storage [m³] and discharge [m³/s] continuity; use the H–V relationship and consistent initial stage/storage.

Metrics describe different errors

NSE expresses squared-error reduction relative to the observed mean, and RMSE gives error in original units. Volume bias describes over/under-export, while peaks and arrival timing describe flood response. Use matched valid timestamps and declare missing-data handling, period and units. Multi-site reporting differs from simultaneous optimization of every parameter.

RMSE = √[Σ(Qsim−Qobs)²/N]; PBIAS = 100 Σ(Qsim−Qobs)/ΣQobs

This illustrative PBIAS convention is positive for overprediction; check the convention in the actual report.

Separate calibration, validation and computational optimization

Calibration selects parameters to reproduce observations; independent validation evaluates unused periods/data. Parallel or memory optimization reduces runtime/resource cost rather than calibrating physical response. Record configuration, initial state and data scope separately so the effect of each change remains interpretable.

12 · Consistency of states, output and execution

Warm-up-state reuse versus full restart

Warm-up adjusts initial states; a cache reuses part of that preparation. HotStart resumes the time/state covered by its saved format. Restoring hydrologic state does not imply restoration of every snow, reservoir and hydraulic variable. Compare uninterrupted and restarted results at matched times and verify model/input/format compatibility.

NetCDF is a format, not a guarantee of aligned meshes

Coordinates, dimensions, units, time reference and missing-value markers define array meaning. Watershed cells, river sections and flood grids differ spatially and may have different output intervals. Link plots/maps by actual time/location without changing producer values, and distinguish partial from finalized files.

Parallel efficiency includes dependency and synchronization costs

Upstream/downstream routing and network coupling impose ordering constraints. Parallel work still incurs waiting, memory and I/O costs, and overhead can dominate for small domains. Distinguish the current OMP-focused package from other execution families and measure output consistency and elapsed time on matched workloads/hardware.

Track completion, validation and distribution separately

Completion of the 1D river and 2D flood solvers describes implementation. Suitability for a particular basin depends on data, boundaries, resolution and observational evaluation. Officially downloadable files follow the actual MyWater release and terms. Ongoing application validation is therefore not a reason to relabel a completed solver as unfinished.

Coupled analysis of river flow continuing after rainfall ends

A 2017 conference presentation proposed coupling K-DRUM with MODFLOW for long-term water-cycle and low-flow analysis.
